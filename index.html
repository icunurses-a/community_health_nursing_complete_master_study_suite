Case 1 • Lecture 4 [Slide 159-160] Family Nursing Process

The Al-Hassan Family Clinical Assessment

Scenario: Two parents, three children, and a grandmother living nearby. One child has asthma with 2 emergency visits in 6 months. Mother manages all medications and reports severe fatigue. Father works long hours and cannot attend clinic visits. Family seeks fewer ER visits but cannot maintain home routines.

1. Immediate Strengths: Intact nuclear family with nearby extended support (grandmother), genuine desire to improve child health.

2. Contributing Dynamics (Family as System): Child's uncontrolled asthma → maternal sleep fatigue → father role strain working extra → collapsed routine → missed inhalers → ER visit → crisis cycle.

3. Nursing Care Plan (Measurable Goal): "Child will experience zero asthma ER visits over the next 6 months through consistent adherence to a shared family asthma action plan."

4. Concrete Interventions: Teach asthma spacer technique to both parents and grandmother; synchronize refills via digital Sehhaty app; establish a visible morning/evening reminder chart.

1. Concepts of Community, Population, and Aggregate

[Slides 3–7 • Rector p. 82–90]

A thorough understanding of community health nursing begins with distinguishing the core structural units of client focus:

Community

A collection of people who share some important feature of their lives, interact, have common interests or characteristics, and have a sense of unity or belonging.

Functions: Collective sense of belonging, shared identity, values, norms, communication, and shared concerns (p. 82).

Population

All of the people occupying an area or all of those who share one or more characteristics. In contrast to community, people in a population do NOT necessarily interact or share a sense of belonging.

Scope: Defined geographically (city population) or by common qualities/demographics (elderly adults) (p. 90).

Aggregate

A mass or grouping of distinct individuals who are considered as a whole and who are loosely associated with one another. A broader term including many different-sized groups.

Crucial Principle: Both communities and populations are types of aggregates (e.g. pregnant teens, diabetics) (p. 90).

Three Types of Communities [Slide 4 • Rector p. 84–89]:
1. Geographic Defined by physical/geographic boundaries (e.g., a city, town, or neighborhood).
2. Common-Interest Bound by common interests, goals, or identity (e.g., mosque congregation, professional nursing organization, women with mastectomies).
3. Community of Solution Group of people who come together specifically to solve a common problem affecting all of them (e.g., addressing water pollution).

2. Definition of Health & The Wellness-Illness Continuum

[Slides 8–11 • Rector p. 92–95]
World Health Organization (WHO) Definition:

"Health is defined as a state of complete physical, mental, and social wellbeing and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity." (p. 92, 2022)

The Dynamic Health Continuum (Figure 1-4): A person's relative health is constantly in flux, ranging from Total Disability or Death at one extreme to Optimal Health at the other.

Total Disability / Death Increasing Level of Illness ← → Increasing Level of Wellness Optimal Health
Paradigm Shift: Traditionally, healthcare focused exclusively on acute/chronic disease treatment at the illness end. Modern CHN shifts focus toward the wellness end of the continuum, aligned with Healthy People 2030 and Saudi Vision 2030.

3. The Social-Ecological Model

[Slide 13 • Savage p. 5–9]

Adapted by CTLT from Dahlgren & Whitehead (1991) and Worthman (1999):

1. Individual
Biological, genetics, age, knowledge, personal attitudes
2. Relationship
Family, peers, partners, immediate social circle
3. Community
Schools, workplaces, neighborhoods, physical infrastructure
4. Societal
Broad cultural norms, health policies, legal system, economy
Four Core Model Assumptions:
  1. There are multiple determinants of health.
  2. A population and environmental approach is critical.
  3. Linkages and relationships among the levels are essential.
  4. Multiple strategies across multiple sectors are mandatory to achieve outcomes. Communities with fewer resources & inadequate infrastructure face significantly elevated disease risks.

5. Community/Public Health Nursing Focus & 8 Core Concepts

[Slides 20–28 • Rector p. 116–131]

Definition: C/PHN combines nursing science with public health science to formulate a community-based and population-focused practice.

Focus Comparison (Basic Bedside vs. C/PHN) [Slide 22]:

Basic hospital nursing allocates ~100% of its initial focus to individuals, declining sharply when moving through families, groups, and reaching 0% for populations. In contrast, Community/Public Health Nursing allocates its highest focus (~100%) to aggregates and populations.

1. Population Focused Nurses do not view individuals separately, but in the context of the community.
2. Greatest Good Services provided to multiple/overlapping groups (e.g. flu vaccine campaigns for all).
3. Equal Partners Involve community at every stage from planning to evaluation to build autonomy.
4. Primary Prevention First-order priority; promotion of healthy habits prioritized over rehabilitation.
5. Thriving Conditions Advocacy and policy changes to build environments where populations thrive.
6. Actively Reaching Out Do not wait for clients in clinics; actively seek populations in rural/home areas.
7. Optimal Resources Promote cost-effective, efficient long-term community health strategies.
8. Interprofessional Cross-sector collaboration to prevent service duplication and care fragmentation.

LECTURE 2 • COMMUNITY ASSESSMENT, DIAGNOSIS, PLANNING & INTERVENTION
Slides 39–81 Ms. Nawal Almaheyani (1448-2026)

Community Assessment, Diagnosis & Health Planning

Primary sources: Savage (2020) p. 77–123 & Rector (2022) p. 1270–1333

1. Community Health Assessment Definition & Purpose

[Slide 42–43 • Savage p. 77–79]

A community health assessment is a strategic plan that describes the health of a community by collecting, analyzing, and using data to:

1. Educate & Mobilize
2. Develop Priorities
3. Obtain Resources
4. Plan Action Programs

*Provides baseline data (e.g. maternal & infant health status) needed before allocating community resources.

2. Six Types of Community Health Assessments

[Slides 44–50 • Savage p. 79–82]
1. Comprehensive Assessment
Collection of data about populations, assets (health department capacity), unmet needs, health disparities, and action opportunities.
2. Population-Focused Assessment
Focuses on a specific group (e.g. pregnant women, immigrants, elderly, or children with developmental disabilities).
3. Setting-Specific Assessment
Assesses organization strengths/weaknesses (e.g., worksite occupational hazards, school health policies, or building air quality).
4. Problem- or Health-Issue-Based Assessment
Targeted analysis using web toolkits on a single issue (e.g., assessing community obesity environmental determinants).
5. Health Impact Assessments (HIA)
Evaluates potential health effects of large societal projects/policies before adoption (e.g., municipal zoning laws promoting walking trails).
6. Rapid Needs Assessment
Straightforward post-disaster data collection conducted within the first week. First used internationally in the 1960s.

3. Assessment Methods & Data Sources

[Slides 51–64 • Rector p. 1298–1305]
Method Description & Purpose Key Features / Advantages Limitations / Examples
1. Surveys Predetermined questions via questionnaires, phone, or interviews. Cost-effective; samples large populations; allows inferences. Relies on self-report accuracy.
2. Descriptive Epidemiology Examines amount and distribution of health events. Assesses: Person (Who?), Place (Where?), and Time (When?). Identifies high-risk groups for targeted programs.
3. GIS Analysis Spatial mapping combining medical geography and epidemiology. Visualizes disparities, resource deficits, environmental exposures. e.g. New Jersey infant outcomes & Worcester pollution/heart attacks.
4. Community Forums Town hall meetings in local halls/gyms to obtain qualitative opinions. Relatively inexpensive; rapid public feedback. Drawback: Only the most vocal/vested voices are heard.
5. Focus Groups Most common method: 6 to 8 homogeneous participants; lasts 1–2 hours. Unstructured open-ended questions explore root beliefs. e.g., focus group of young mothers or retired businessmen.
Primary, Secondary & Saudi Data Sources [Slides 60–63]:
Primary vs. Secondary: Primary = directly talking to people/records; Secondary = records created during routine work (needs validation).
Saudi National Sources: GASTAT (stats.gov.sa), MOH (moh.gov.sa), Health Data Platform HDP (hdp.moh.gov.sa), National Surveys, Saudi Census.
Local Regional Sources: Regional Health Directorates, PHCs, Municipalities (Baladiyah for sanitation/housing), Schools, Community NGOs.

4. Community Diagnoses (4-Part Statement)

[Slides 65–67 • Savage p. 116–117]

A community diagnosis summarizes findings to direct program planning. It contains exactly four parts:

1. The Problem
2. The Population
3. Related To (Etiology)
4. Demonstrated By (Data)

5. Health Planning, Implementation & Evaluation

[Slides 68–76 • Rector p. 1316–1333]
Health Planning (4 Stages)
1. Priority setting.
2. Establishing goals and objectives.
3. Implementing health promotion plans.
4. Evaluating implemented programs.
Implementation (5 Stages)
1. Community accepting the program.
2. Specifying tasks and needed resources.
3. Developing activity plans.
4. Establishing management mechanism.
5. Putting the plan into action.
Evaluation Dichotomy
Formative: Process during intervention (e.g. rescheduling class to morning).
Summative: Outcome impact (e.g. factory PPE adherence +15%).
6 Characteristics of the Nursing Process in CHN [Slides 77–80]:
1. Deliberative: Thoughtful, independent decision-making.
2. Adaptable: Flexible to unique aggregate situations.
3. Cyclical: Continuous loop of assessment → feedback.
4. Client-Focused: Designed explicitly for population health.
5. Interactive: Ongoing bidirectional communication.
6. Need-Oriented: From disease treatment to promotion.
LECTURE 3 • PRIMARY HEALTH CARE (PHC) & SAUDI HEALTH TRANSFORMATION
Slides 82–124 Dr. Aishah Al Harbi (1448H-2026)

Primary Health Care & Saudi Vision 2030 Modern Care Model

WHO Alma-Ata Declaration, Savage (2020) p. 374–383 & Saudi MOH Guidelines

1. Definition and 4 Principles of PHC

[Slides 86–88 • WHO & Savage p. 374]

WHO Alma-Ata Definition: Essential health care based on practical, scientifically sound, and socially acceptable methods and technology, universally accessible through full community participation, at an affordable cost in a spirit of self-reliance.

1. Community Participation Voluntary involvement where members actively identify needs, plan, organize, and implement services.
2. Intersectoral Coordination Involvement of education, housing, water, transport, public, and private sectors.
3. Appropriate Technologies Accessible, scientifically sound, locally adaptable, and acceptable to staff/clients.
4. Support Mechanisms Adequately trained, well-distributed healthcare workforce with local & referral backup.
Eight Basic Components of PHC [Slide 89]:
  1. Health education on prevailing health problems & prevention/control.
  2. Promotion of food supply and proper nutrition.
  3. Safe water supply and basic sanitation.
  4. Maternal and child health care, including family planning.
  5. Immunization against major infectious diseases.
  6. Prevention and control of locally endemic diseases.
  7. Appropriate treatment of common diseases and injuries.
  8. Provision of essential drugs.

2. Immunization Science & Saudi National Schedule

[Slides 93–100 • Savage p. 380–381]
Vaccine Types:
  • Live-Attenuated: Weakened live germ; mimics natural infection, produces strong long-lasting response (e.g., BCG, MMR, Varicella, OPV, Rotavirus).
  • Inactivated: Killed version; protection weaker; requires boosters (e.g., HepA, Inactivated Polio IPV, Influenza, Rabies).
  • Subunit / Conjugate: Specific antigen fragments (protein/sugar); highly targeted strong response (e.g., Hib, PCV, HepB, HPV).
  • Toxoid: Inactivated bacterial toxin; teaches immune system to fight toxin (e.g., Diphtheria, Tetanus).
Administration Routes:
  • ID (Intradermal): BCG vaccine (at birth).
  • IM (Intramuscular): HepB, DTaP, Hib, PCV, IPV, MCV4, HepA, HPV, Tdap.
  • SC (Subcutaneous): IPV, Measles, Varicella, MMR.
  • Oral: Rotavirus (RV), Oral Polio Vaccine (OPV).
Saudi National Schedule Highlights [Slide 100]:

Birth: BCG & HepB • 2, 4, 6 Months: HepB, IPV, DTaP, Hib, PCV, RV • 9 Months: Measles & MCV4 • 12 Months: MMR, PCV, OPV, MCV4 • 18 Months: DTaP, Hib, OPV, MMR, Varicella, HepA • 24 Months: HepA • Primary School Entry (6 yrs): OPV, DTaP, MMR, Varicella • Grade 4 (girls): HPV • Grade 10 / 16-18 yrs: Tdap & MCV4 • Special groups: Seasonal flu, Hajj pilgrims, high-risk travelers.

3. Secondary, Tertiary Care & Case Management

[Slides 101–107 • Savage p. 381–383]
Secondary Prevention in PHC Focuses on identifying individuals with subclinical disease and initiating prompt treatment via screening (BP screening, mammography, cancer screening) to reduce morbidity and mortality.
Tertiary Prevention & Case Management Minimizes complications and sequelae of Noncommunicable Diseases (NCDs). Case manager coordinates options to reduce duplication, control costs, and improve holistic patient outcomes.

4. Saudi Vision 2030 Healthcare Sector Transformation

[Slides 109–121 • Saudi MOH]

The Modern Healthcare Model shifts care from curative facilities to comprehensive, person-centered prevention across six integrated care systems:

1. Preventive Care Healthy lifestyle adoption and early community risk screening.
2. Urgent Care Emergency access at the right time and right facility.
3. Elective Care Optimizing scheduled surgeries with full pre- and post-op support.
4. Safe Childbirth Continuous support from pre-marital testing through postnatal care.
5. Chronic Disease Care Proactive management pathways to avert complications.
6. Palliative Care Compassionate support for terminal illness and grieving families.
Four Customer Triaging Pathways in Saudi PHCs [Slide 116–120]:
1. Family Medicine Journey
2. Maternity & Well Baby
3. Dentistry Journey
4. Urgent Care Pathway
LECTURE 4 • FAMILY AS CLIENT & FAMILY HEALTH NURSING
Slides 125–163 Dr. Norah G. Alkhaledi (1448H-2026)

Family as Client & Family Health Nursing

Robinson et al. (2022) 7th Ed. & Potter et al. (2013) 8th Ed.

1. Family Definitions & Rationales

[Slides 127–131 • Rector p. 1146–1150]
Family Definition: Two or more individuals sharing a residence or living near one another; possess emotional bonds; engage in interrelated social positions, roles, and tasks; and share cultural ties and affection.
Family Health: A dynamic changing state of wellbeing involving biological, psychological, spiritual, sociological, and cultural factors of individual members and the whole family unit.
Why Is the Family a Nursing Client? Family members directly influence one another's habits and health outcomes; illness alters family routines, finances, and roles; and the family serves as the primary caregiver and support system.

2. Family Structures & Characteristics of a Healthy Family

[Slides 132–135 • Rector p. 1152–1161]
Nuclear Family Husband, wife, and their biological or adopted children.
Extended Family Nuclear family plus blood relatives: grandparents, aunts, uncles, cousins.
Blended Family Married couple and offspring including children from prior marriages.
Single-Parent Divorce, separation, death, or choice (90% headed by single mothers).
8 Characteristics of a Healthy Family [Slide 134]:
1. Communicate & listen well.
2. Support & affirm members.
3. Respect & trust.
4. Balanced interactions.
5. Share leisure time.
6. Shared responsibilities.
7. Shared religious core.
8. Seek help in difficulty.

3. Three Perspectives & Family-Centered Care

[Slides 136–141 • Potter 2013 • Robinson 2022]
A. Family as Context Individual is primary client; nurse examines how family supports the individual (e.g. parents giving inhaler).
B. Family as Client The family unit as a whole is assessed (e.g. recurring family conflict regarding diabetic dietary routines).
C. Family as System Dynamic feedback loops: change in one member reverberates across all members and the entire household.

4. Visual Assessment Tools: Genogram & Ecomap

[Slides 151–156 • WHO 2016]
Genogram (Pedigree Chart) Visual representation of family structure across at least three generations. Uses standard symbols (squares for males, circles for females, slashed lines for deceased) to chart biological hereditary risks and relationship patterns.
Ecomap (Ecological Map) Diagrams the family's connections with external systems (school, hospital, mosque, workplace, neighbors). Line types indicate strength: solid (strong), dotted (weak), slashed (stressful), arrows indicate energy/support flow.

LECTURE 6 • HEALTH PROMOTION, PLANNED CHANGE & COMMUNITY MOBILIZATION
Slides 195–234 Dr. Manal Abo Elmagd & Dr. Muna Alharbi (2026)

Health Promotion, Planned Change & Community Mobilization

Rector (2022) Ch. 11 (p. 943–957), 2018 (p. 1056–1057) & Open University Framework

1. Health Promotion vs. Disease Prevention

[Slides 197–204 • Rector p. 943]
Health Promotion Behaviors that improve overall well-being and lead to a desire to meet one's full human potential. Focuses on moving people closer to optimal wellness without a pre-existing disease (e.g. teaching balanced diet, corporate fitness).
Disease Prevention (Health Protection) Behavior motivated by a desire to actively avoid illness, detect it early, or maintain functioning within the constraints of illness.
WHO 3-Pronged Health Promotion Goals [Slide 200]: 1. Increase span of healthy life for all citizens • 2. Reduce health disparities among groups • 3. Achieve universal access to preventive services.

2. Nature of Change & Lewin's 3 Sequential Stages

[Slides 205–211 • Rector p. 946–948]
Evolutionary Change (Gradual Reform) Gradual, incremental modifications that do not replace current operations (e.g., becoming parents, tapering cigarettes daily, cutting desserts).
Revolutionary Change (Sudden/Drastic) Rapid, threatening shift requiring radically new patterns without warning (e.g., sudden job loss, overnight smoking cessation, emergency child removal).
1. Unfreezing
Disequilibrium develops; people feel a void or dissatisfaction. Change is initiated either intrinsically or by external forces.
2. Changing / Moving
People examine, try, and accept the innovation through attitude transformation. Change agent demonstrates value.
3. Refreezing
Change is permanently integrated and stabilized into habits to avoid the natural tendency to relapse.

3. Planned-Change 8 Steps & Chin-Benne Strategies

[Slides 212–219 • Rector p. 950–954]
8 Steps of the Planned-Change Process:
1. Recognize symptoms → 2. Diagnose need → 3. Analyze alternatives → 4. Select change → 5. Plan change → 6. Implement → 7. Evaluate → 8. Stabilize
Six Principles for Effecting Positive Change:
  1. 1. Participation: Involve stakeholders in all steps.
  2. 2. Resistance to Change: Anticipate natural homeostasis.
  3. 3. Proper Timing: Postpone if timing is counterproductive.
  4. 4. Interdependence: Changing one part affects whole system.
  5. 5. Flexibility: Adapt gracefully to unforeseen barriers.
  6. 6. Self-Understanding: Nurse understands own motives/role.

4. Community Mobilization & 7-Step Action Cycle

[Slides 224–233 • Open University Module 19]

Community Mobilization Definition: A process by which communities are motivated to take action to improve their state of development and well-being, bringing together different societal factions to deal with health crises or social problems.

Techniques to Involve Communities & Opinion Leaders:

Identify opinion leaders and cultural informants who explain habits, customs, values, and taboos (community norms). Health professionals must never dictate; they engage in continuous dialogue while embodying role-model behaviors: polite, patient, good listener, tolerant, honest, and non-judgmental.

The 7-Step Action Cycle (Female Circumcision Case Study) [Slide 232]:
  1. Step 1: Identify significant health problem (e.g. female circumcision complications).
  2. Step 2: Plan and select a strategy (e.g. conduct sensitization workshop for leaders).
  3. Step 3: Identify key actors and stakeholders (village chief, Imam, heads of families).
  4. Step 4: Mobilize key actors for collaborative action.
